## Overview 

Until the iPhone 18 Pro, Apple's iPhone main cameras used fixed apertures. You could blur a background in software, but the actual hole letting light into the lens never moved. That changes with the **iPhone 18 Pro** and **iPhone 18 Pro Max**, which introduce a mechanical variable aperture on the 48MP Fusion main camera — a first for the iPhone line, arriving years after Samsung introduced a variable-aperture system on the Galaxy S9 and S10.

## What actually changed

The iPhone 17 Pro's main camera was fixed at ƒ/1.78. The 18 Pro replaces that with a six-blade iris — Apple says each blade is thinner than a human hair — that physically opens and closes across four aperture settings, from ƒ/1.48 to ƒ/4.0. Apple's own explanation, given to PetaPixel, is refreshingly practical: pushing the lens to a faster ƒ/1.48 for better low light meant risking blown-out focus in group shots at close range, so a variable iris became the fix rather than the headline feature.

| Setting | Aperture | Useful for |
|---|---|---|
| Wide open | ƒ/1.48 | Low light, stronger background blur |
| Default | ƒ/1.8 | General shooting |
| Mid-stop | ƒ/2.8 | More depth of field |
| Narrow | ƒ/4.0 | Group shots and scenes where you want more of the frame in focus |

The practical benefit isn't simply "more light" or "less light." Aperture also changes depth of field. At ƒ/1.48, the camera can create stronger separation between a subject and its background, while stopping down toward ƒ/4.0 keeps more of a group or nearby scene within the zone of acceptable focus. That's the part a fixed-aperture phone camera cannot reproduce optically, no matter how good the software-based blur has gotten.

The new Pro Controls also add manual adjustment for aperture, shutter speed, white balance, and other exposure controls, with a histogram for checking exposure. The 48MP ultra-wide and 48MP telephoto remain in the system, and the telephoto retains 8x optical-quality zoom (up from 5x on the 17 Pro). The main camera, however, gets both the new variable aperture and a new sensor, and both the 6.3-inch 18 Pro and the 6.9-inch 18 Pro Max use the same main-camera system, so size no longer dictates camera capability the way it used to.

## The fragility question nobody's answering yet

A moving mechanical part inside a phone that gets dropped, pocketed, and exposed to moisture is a legitimate long-term question. Apple says the six aperture blades were designed with durability in mind, but launch-period coverage can't tell us how the mechanism will behave after years of daily use. That's something independent long-term testing will need to establish.

Variable aperture isn't unique to Apple, either. Several Android manufacturers have used mechanically adjustable apertures in flagship phones, though implementation and available controls vary — see our breakdown of [Oppo's 2026 camera lineup](https://www.revibyte.blog/oppo/oppo-2026-camera-lineup-what-really-changes/) for how one competitor approaches it differently.

## Who should care

If you shoot mostly in Auto and never touch manual controls, the day-to-day difference may be smaller than the spec sheet suggests — computational photography was already compensating for a fixed aperture reasonably well. Where this matters is genuinely mixed lighting: outdoor group shots that used to soften faces at the edges of frame, or dim indoor scenes where the ƒ/1.48 setting can admit roughly 50% more light than the old ƒ/1.78 aperture, according to Apple's stated figures and current reporting.

## FAQ

**Does the variable aperture work automatically, or do I have to set it myself?**
Both. Auto mode picks the aperture based on the scene, but you can override it manually in the Camera app by tapping one of the four aperture dots.

**Is the variable aperture on the ultra-wide or front camera too?**
No. Apple has only confirmed it on the 48MP Fusion main camera. The ultra-wide, telephoto, and front camera keep fixed apertures.

**Does the bigger iPhone 18 Pro Max get a better camera than the standard 18 Pro?**
Not for the main sensor. Apple confirmed both models share the same main camera and variable aperture system; the size difference is primarily screen and battery, not optics.

**Is a mechanical aperture actually worth it, or is this a gimmick?**
It appears useful rather than merely cosmetic, particularly when you need more depth of field or want more control over exposure for video. PetaPixel's hands-on testing found sharpness actually peaks at ƒ/2.8, with the wide-open ƒ/1.48 setting noticeably softer and ƒ/4.0 dipping slightly from diffraction — which helps explain why Apple didn't simply lock the camera to its widest setting. Early hands-on impressions point to real benefits, but longer-term testing is still needed before judging durability and how much difference it makes for typical everyday shooting.
