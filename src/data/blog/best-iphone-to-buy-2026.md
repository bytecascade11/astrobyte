## Overview 

Every year the "which iPhone should I buy" question gets a little more annoying to answer, because Apple keeps stretching the lineup thinner. You're no longer picking between two or three phones — you're picking between a base model, an ultra-thin Air, a Pro, a Pro Max, and whatever's left of last year's models still sitting on shelves at a discount. In 2026, that gap has only gotten wider, and the "best" iPhone genuinely depends on what you're optimizing for: price, camera output, battery life, or just not wanting to think about it for the next four years.  
  
After comparing available benchmarks, retailer listings, and real-world review data across the current lineup — rather than just repeating spec sheets — here's where each model actually makes sense, with Nigerian pricing included, since forex swings hit naira prices harder than almost anywhere else.

The gap between the base model and the Pro tier has narrowed this generation, but it hasn't closed. All four current models now run ProMotion up to 120Hz, which used to be a Pro-exclusive feature — that alone makes the base iPhone 17 feel noticeably smoother than older base models. Where they still diverge is peak brightness in direct sunlight (a real factor if you're outdoors a lot in Lagos or Abuja heat) and screen size options: the Pro Max's 6.9-inch panel is meaningfully larger than the 6.3-inch base model and even the 6.5-inch Air.  
  
On the chip side, only the base iPhone 17 runs the standard A19 — the Air, Pro, and Pro Max all ship with the A19 Pro. That said, the Air gets a slightly cut-down version with one less GPU core than the Pro and Pro Max, and it lacks their vapor chamber cooling, so it can't sustain heavy loads as long before throttling kicks in. For everyday browsing, social media, and casual gaming, none of this is noticeable — it only shows up in sustained tasks like long gaming sessions, 4K video editing, or extended ProRAW shooting bursts.  
  
Storage-wise, the base model tops out lower than the Pro tier, and the Pro Max is the only model offering a 2TB option — relevant if you shoot a lot of 4K video and don't want to lean on cloud storage, which isn't always reliable on Nigerian mobile data.

## Charging and Battery: Don't Sleep on the Numbers  
  
Charging speeds are similar across the lineup, with MagSafe wireless charging at 25W on the base, Pro, and Pro Max, and a slightly slower 20W on the Air due to its slim battery. What actually matters more day-to-day is raw battery capacity: the Air's slim profile comes at the cost of the smallest battery in the lineup, while the Pro Max carries the largest cell and consistently delivers the longest screen-on time in the range. In a market where power outages are common and topping up isn't always convenient, that gap is worth weighing seriously. We cover this in more depth in our dedicated [best iPhones for battery life](https://www.revibyte.blog/posts/best-iphones-battery-life/) guide.  
  
## Who Should Buy the Base iPhone 17  
  
If your current phone still works but feels sluggish, the standard iPhone 17 is the sensible middle ground. It carries over most of what made last year's Pro feel premium — smoother display motion, a genuinely capable main sensor, and enough processing headroom to not feel behind for a few years. It's not the phone for someone chasing the best possible zoom shots, but for messaging, social media, and casual photography, it holds its own without the Pro markup — and at current Nigerian pricing, it's the model that makes the most financial sense for most buyers.

## Long-Term Support: Which iPhone Ages Better  
  
Apple typically supports iPhones with major iOS updates for around 6-7 years from release, and that support window is identical across the base, Air, Pro, and Pro Max within the same generation — so buying the base model doesn't mean losing software updates sooner. What does age differently is real-world performance: the A19 Pro chip in the Pro and Pro Max tier has more headroom to stay smooth several years down the line compared to the standard A19, which matters if you plan to hold onto the phone for four or five years rather than upgrading every cycle.

## Buying a Used iPhone in Nigeria: What to Check  
  
A large share of iPhones sold in Nigeria are imported UK or US used units, and that's a perfectly reasonable way to get current-gen hardware at a lower price — as long as you check the phone properly before paying. Computer Village and similar markets have plenty of legitimate sellers, but also enough bad actors that a quick inspection routine is worth the ten minutes it takes.  
  
![Checking an iPhone's settings and IMEI before buying used](/assets/posts/iphone-imei-check-settings.jpg)  
  
- **Check battery health.** Go to Settings > Battery > Battery Health & Charging and confirm the maximum capacity is above 85%. Anything lower means you'll be charging more often than you'd like within a year.  
- **Check Face ID works properly.** Set it up on the spot and test it in different lighting, including low light. A phone with a damaged Face ID module is a common issue on used units and expensive to fix.  
- **Check True Tone is available.** If True Tone is missing or grayed out in Display settings, it usually means the screen has been replaced with a non-genuine part — a sign the phone has had prior repairs, possibly hiding other issues.  
- **Check for iCloud lock.** Go to Settings > General > About or ask the seller to sign out of iCloud in front of you. If they hesitate or can't remove Find My iPhone, walk away — you could end up with a bricked device.  
- **Verify the IMEI.** Dial `*#06#` or check Settings > General > About for the IMEI, then confirm it on Apple's official coverage checker to make sure the phone isn't reported lost, stolen, or still under someone else's Find My account.  
- **Avoid unusually cheap units.** If a price looks far below every other listing for the same model and storage, there's almost always a reason — a blacklisted IMEI, a hidden repair, or a phone that isn't what it's advertised as.  
  
Buying from a seller who lets you do all of the above before payment is usually a good sign in itself. If they rush you or refuse a proper check, that's worth treating as a red flag on its own.

Where you buy matters almost as much as which model you buy, since it directly affects whether you get a warranty, a genuine device, and someone to go back to if something goes wrong.  
  
- **Authorized retailers and Apple resellers** are the safest option for brand-new units. You pay a premium over street prices, but you get a real warranty, verified authenticity, and proper after-sales support — worth it for anyone who isn't confident inspecting a phone themselves.  
- **Trusted online stores** with verifiable business history, clear return policies, and consistent customer reviews are a solid middle ground, especially for buyers outside Lagos or Abuja who can't easily visit a physical shop. Look for stores that have been active for years, not ones that popped up recently with prices that seem too good to be true.  
- **Reputable sellers at Computer Village and similar markets** can offer genuinely good deals on both new and used iPhones, but reputation there is everything — buy from vendors with a track record, physical shop presence, and a willingness to let you run the checks covered in the used-iPhone section above before you pay.  
- **Avoid random social media sellers** you have no way to verify. A deal that lands in your DMs or WhatsApp from someone with no reviews, no fixed location, and pressure to pay quickly is one of the most common ways people end up with a blacklisted, locked, or outright fake device in Nigeria. If you can't inspect the phone in person or verify the seller independently, it's not worth the risk no matter how good the price looks.  
  
Whichever route you go, the fundamentals from the previous section still apply — check the battery health, Face ID, True Tone, iCloud lock, and IMEI before you hand over money.  
  
## FAQ  
  
**Is the iPhone 17 Pro Max worth the extra cost over the regular Pro?**  
Only if battery life or the extended zoom range genuinely matters to your daily use. For most people, the difference is noticeable but not essential — and at Nigerian pricing, that gap can be several hundred thousand naira.  
  
**Should I buy last year's iPhone instead of the newest one?**  
If the price drop is significant and you don't need the latest camera or chip improvements, buying the previous generation is often the smarter financial move, especially with naira volatility pushing current-gen prices higher.  
  
**Does the base iPhone 17 support the same camera features as the Pro?**  
No — the Pro models get additional lenses and computational photography features the base model doesn't have, though the main sensor on the standard model is still solid for everyday shooting.  
  
**Which iPhone has the best battery life in 2026?**  
The Pro Max leads in real-world battery stamina thanks to its larger cell, followed by the Pro. The Air, due to its slim design, has the smallest battery in the current lineup.  
  
**Why do iPhone prices in Nigeria vary so much between retailers?**  
Because most units are imported, pricing tracks the daily USD/NGN exchange rate plus shipping, customs duties, and individual retailer margins — the same model can legitimately differ by hundreds of thousands of naira between vendors.  
  
**Is it better to buy an iPhone or a flagship Android in 2026?**  
It depends on ecosystem preference and budget more than raw hardware at this point — both platforms have genuinely competitive flagships this year.
